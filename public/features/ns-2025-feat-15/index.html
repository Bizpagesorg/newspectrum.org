Barclays' £8.1 Billion Rebound: Record Pay, a £50 Billion Shadow and a Bank Refighting for Position

Published: Feb 13, 2025

Barclays closed 2024 with pre-tax profits of £8.1 billion, up 24% from £6.6 billion a year earlier, propelled by a rebound in investment banking and steady interest rates that supported its United Kingdom retail business. The results, published in February 2025, did more than flatter shareholders: they lifted chief executive CS Venkatakrishnan's pay to £10.5 million, expanded the bonus pool to nearly £1.9 billion, and set the bank's biggest single-year remuneration headline since Bob Diamond's era - all while the lender took its first-ever provision for the motor finance commission scandal and absorbed a two-day technology outage. This is a dissection of what the 2024 accounts reveal about the anatomy of a modern universal bank in recovery.

The numbers behind the rebound

The headline mechanics of Barclays' 2024 are straightforward. Pre-tax profit rose almost a quarter to £8.1 billion. Two engines did the work. The first was the investment bank, where income jumped 7% amid a rebound in dealmaking and market activity - the cyclical businesses of advising on mergers, underwriting securities and trading that had been depressed across the industry since the 2022-2023 rate shock froze issuance. The second was the domestic UK franchise, where steady interest rates supported income: after two years of aggressive Bank of England tightening, the deposit and lending spreads that British banks earn remained at levels unseen since the financial crisis.

What makes the 24% increase analytically interesting is not its size but its composition. Barclays is the only major British lender whose profit center spans both sides of the Atlantic - a Wall Street-capable investment bank bolted onto a UK high-street retail operation. In 2023 that structure was a drag: weak investment banking offset strong net interest income. In 2024 the two engines fired together, which is precisely the scenario the dual model promises and rarely delivers. The rebound in dealmaking and market activity was industry-wide, but Barclays' operating leverage on it was unusually high because its fixed cost base in the investment bank had been kept intact through the downturn in the expectation of exactly this recovery.

A chief executive's pay packet as a signal

The remuneration disclosures turned the results into a national debate. CS Venkatakrishnan's payout rose from £4.6 million in 2023 to £10.5 million - the highest package paid to a Barclays CEO in a single year since Bob Diamond's controversial £17 million in 2011. The mechanics matter: the jump came from the vesting of long-term bonuses and from the rise in the value of Barclays shares that make up those payouts, according to the bank's annual report. In other words, the largest component of the increase was not a new grant but the market's own re-rating of the bank arriving, with a multi-year lag, in the chief executive's account.

Shareholders were asked to approve a new pay policy at the 2025 annual general meeting that cuts Venkatakrishnan's salary but increases his potential bonus, giving him the chance to earn up to £14.3 million a year. The design reflects a decade of investor pressure to shift executive pay toward performance-contingent variable components. It also raises the ceiling: a bank that just paid its CEO the most since 2011 is proposing a structure under which he could earn 40% more.

The bonus pool and the 832

Below the chief executive, the distribution widened in parallel. Top performers shared a bonus pool worth nearly £1.9 billion, up from £1.7 billion a year earlier. Within it sits the disclosure that generated the most scrutiny: £647 million of bonuses shared between 832 so-called "material risk takers" inside the investment bank, who in total earned £1.2 billion between them. One unidentified banker took home more than €17 million (£14 million) - a third more than the chief executive's own package, according to documents filed alongside the annual report.

The material-risk-taker category is a European regulatory construct: senior bankers, traders and executives whose decisions can move the firm's risk profile. Their identification is mandatory, their pay is scrutinized, and under EU rules their variable remuneration is capped relative to fixed pay - a cap the UK retained in modified form. The 2024 figures show the category functioning as designed: when investment banking income rebounds 7% and share prices rise, the population whose pay is most tightly geared to those outcomes captures the swing first.

The political reaction was immediate. Luke Hildyard, executive director of the High Pay Centre thinktank, said the £10 million payout for the Barclays CEO and the £1.2 billion spent on a few hundred investment bankers epitomise the inequality in the UK economy, with excess at the top contrasting with much wider hardship across a country plagued by stagnant growth and the aftermath of the cost of living crisis. Whether or not one accepts the framing, it marks the distance between the bank's internal logic - pay follows revenue and share price - and the external environment of a UK economy that had only just escaped recession when the results landed.

From chief risk officer to the top job

Understanding Venkatakrishnan's Barclays requires his trajectory. He took over as chief executive in November 2021 after Jes Staley stepped down unexpectedly amid an investigation by the City regulator into whether he had properly disclosed his ties to the late financier and child sexual abuse offender Jeffrey Epstein. Venkatakrishnan had served as Barclays chief risk officer from 2016 to 2020, having joined from Wall Street bank JP Morgan, where he had worked since 1994 in senior roles in asset management and investment banking.

The profile - risk officer turned chief executive, an immigrant born in India leading one of Britain's most global banks - shaped both his mandate and the year's cultural flashpoints. He was hired to steady a franchise whose leadership had just been removed by a conduct scandal, and the 2024 result is, in that reading, the completion of the stabilization: profits recovered to £8.1 billion, the investment bank re-armed, the share price strong enough to lift deferred pay into eight figures.

Reading the 24% from three directions

The same result supports three different readings, and each implies a different 2025. Read cyclically, 2024 was a normalization year: dealmaking recovered from its post-2022 freeze, market activity returned, and a bank carrying fixed investment-banking costs converted the recovery into operating leverage. If that reading is right, 2025 depends on the deal pipeline rather than on Barclays itself.

Read structurally, the year validated the transatlantic model at precisely the moment British banking debated its future: a UK retail franchise earning crisis-era spreads plus a US-capable investment bank that can harvest a dealmaking cycle its domestic rivals cannot touch. If that reading is right, the strategic debate shifts from whether Barclays should keep its Wall Street arm to how much capital it deserves.

Read politically, the year is a story about distribution: £8.1 billion of profit, £1.9 billion of bonuses and £10.5 million for one executive, announced into an economy where the High Pay Centre could credibly contrast boardroom excess with stagnant growth and cost-of-living aftermath. If that reading dominates, the binding constraint on Barclays in 2025 is not revenue but legitimacy - expressed through AGM votes, tax debates and the chancellor's interventions in the motor finance litigation.

Why the PPI comparison matters

The reference point for every British banker over fifty is payment protection insurance: a mis-selling scandal that accumulated tens of billions of pounds in redress over more than a decade, long after the practice stopped. The motor finance judgment echoes that pattern in three ways - a practice that was standard while it lasted, a legal turning point that redefined it as unfair after the fact, and a compensation horizon measured in years rather than quarters. It differs in one decisive way: PPI redress ran through a regulatory scheme with a deadline, while motor finance compensation is being shaped by courts and by possible government intervention, which makes the tail both longer and less predictable. Barclays exited motor finance in late 2019; PPI's history says that exiting early reduces the bill but rarely eliminates it.

The motor finance provision: £90 million against a £50 billion shadow

The profit jump came despite the bank putting aside its first-ever provision for the growing scandal around motor finance commissions, worth £90 million. The provision is small against the headline, but the exposure it acknowledges is potentially the largest in British consumer credit history.

Lenders have been grappling with the fallout of a court judgment in October 2024 that vastly expanded a Financial Conduct Authority investigation. The ruling determined that paying a "secret" commission to car dealers who arranged loans - without disclosing the sum and terms of that commission to borrowers - was unlawful. Dealers acted as credit brokers and were paid by lenders for arranging finance; the court found that concealing those payments from the customer breached the duty of fairness. The judgment ignited fears of a compensation bill that could rival the payment protection insurance scandal and cost lenders a combined £50 billion.

Barclays' position within that exposure is unusually defensive: the bank pulled out of the motor finance market in late 2019, before the practice became the center of litigation. Yet exit does not equal immunity - legacy books remain actionable, and analysts at RBC Capital expect Barclays could still face a compensation bill of up to £442 million, nearly five times the provision taken. Banks are now hoping that efforts by the chancellor, Rachel Reeves, to intervene in the case and potentially sway the courts toward lower payouts could cut their bills. The gap between the £90 million provision and the £442 million analyst estimate is the market's measure of how unresolved the liability remains.

The outage and the operational agenda

The results month also carried an operational black eye. Barclays grappled with the fallout of a serious technology outage in February that disrupted its digital services for about two days. Venkatakrishnan apologized to customers affected by the incident: he said he was deeply apologetic for the inconvenience caused, that the bank was absolutely focused on assisting customers who experienced difficulty, and that it would strive to make sure the people impacted would be compensated.

Two days of disrupted digital services is, for a bank whose retail franchise increasingly lives in its app, more than an IT incident - it is a stress test of the proposition that current-account banking has become. The compensation commitment matters because UK regulators have made operational resilience a formal supervisory priority, with banks required to map important business services and set tolerances for disruption. An outage of this length sits inside those frameworks, and the reputational arithmetic of the apology was directed as much at the regulator as at the customer.

The culture wars reach the boardroom

One results-day exchange illustrated how far United States politics had travelled into European banking. Barclays, headquartered in London, was keeping a close eye on developments in the US, where Donald Trump had used executive orders to reverse diversity, equity and inclusion (DEI) policies in the federal government and was trying to apply them to the private sector. While rival financial companies such as Deloitte and Goldman Sachs started to roll back their own DEI policies in response, Venkatakrishnan said Barclays had an enduring commitment to a culture of inclusivity in the bank - enduring and unwavering, in his words.

His argument was framed in business terms rather than political ones: if you want to get the very best people, by necessity you get a very diverse workforce; the bank wants to provide equality of opportunity and create an inclusive environment in which people can shine, excel and contribute. For a bank with a US investment banking footprint, the statement was a calculated position - maintaining a policy stance that its American competitors were retreating from, while its American regulators' political environment was hardening against it.

What the 2024 accounts actually tell you

  • The dual-engine model worked: investment banking income up 7% on a dealmaking rebound, UK retail income supported by steady rates - together lifting pre-tax profit 24% to £8.1bn.
  • Pay followed the cycle with a lag: £10.5m for the CEO, a £1.9bn bonus pool, £1.2bn for 832 material risk takers - the market's re-rating converting into compensation.
  • The largest quantified risk is not on the income statement: a £90m motor finance provision against an analyst-estimated £442m bill and a £50bn industry-wide shadow.
  • Operations remain the soft flank: a two-day digital outage with promised compensation, under formal operational-resilience scrutiny.
  • A new pay policy pending shareholder approval caps salary but raises potential bonus to £14.3m - more performance-linked, more generous at the top.

Outlook: the tests of 2025

The forward agenda writes itself from the accounts. First, the motor finance liability: the gap between provision, analyst estimate and the industry's £50 billion worst case will only be closed by the courts or by the chancellor's intervention, neither of which Barclays controls. Second, the sustainability of the investment banking rebound: a 7% income recovery is a cyclical gift that can be revoked if dealmaking cools again. Third, the pay policy vote at the AGM - a shareholder rebuke would be rare but would land awkwardly on a bank that just printed its biggest CEO package since 2011. Fourth, operational resilience: after a two-day outage, the next incident will be judged against the promise of compensation and the regulator's tolerances.

The 2024 results are best read as a hinge rather than a destination. Barclays demonstrated that its transatlantic model can deliver a simultaneous recovery - something its domestic-focused rivals neither need nor can replicate. It also demonstrated how quickly a profitable year can be colonized by older sins: a £8.1 billion profit story arrived packaged with a motor finance provision, a two-day outage and a £10.5 million pay packet that turned an earnings release into a debate about inequality. The bank that emerged from 2024 is stronger, richer and more exposed to judgment - from shareholders, from regulators, from courts and from the public - than at any point since Venkatakrishnan took the chief executive's chair in November 2021.
