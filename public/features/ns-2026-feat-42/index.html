Russia Rewires University Admissions: Employer-Bound Target Places, 47,000 Paid Seats Cut and a New Quota Ladder

Published: Jul 25, 2026

Russia's 2026 university admissions campaign ended on July 25 with the last day for document submission, and its final results were published on university websites and the Gosuslugi portal on July 27. But the story of this cycle is not only about who got in. Over the past year the state has rebuilt the mechanics of higher-education intake — tying target places to named employers and regions, cutting about 47,000 paid seats in the least priority disciplines, reordering the quota ladder and restricting subsidised education loans to fields the economy needs. Admissions in Russia have become an instrument of labour-market policy, as Kommersant reported from the closing days of the campaign.

A campaign built on labour policy

Parents of applicants complained throughout the summer about the difficulty of winning a state-funded place even with strong results — 240 points and above across three Unified State Exam (EGE) subjects turned out to be insufficient for admission to budget places at the country's most prestigious universities. The reason, interlocutors of Kommersant FM explained, is the large number of olympiad winners and privileged-category applicants who hold priority in admission; even candidates with maximum EGE scores could find themselves at best at the end of the list.

That complaint describes the demand side of a campaign whose supply side the government had already reshaped months earlier. At the start of the year, Valery Falkov (Валерий Фальков), head of the Ministry of Science and Higher Education, announced that Russian universities had cut 47,000 paid places — roughly 13% of the total. The changes mainly affected economics, advertising and public relations, and jurisprudence. In April, the government for the first time distributed target-training places with a binding to a concrete employer and region. And the ministry reworked the order in which unfilled quota places move between categories. Taken together, these decisions turned the 2026 intake into the clearest expression so far of a policy that steers higher education toward the state's list of priority occupations.

The 47,000 paid places that disappeared

The reduction of paid places is the bluntest instrument in the new configuration. Until now universities themselves determined the volume of commercial intake; from this cycle, for non-priority directions — jurisprudence, economics, journalism and others — the ministry limits the number of commercial places. Arseny Filin (Арсений Филин), an education expert and author of the Grand Exam project, notes the consequence directly: the restriction will lead to higher competition and, probably, to higher tuition prices.

The logic of the cut is easy to read from the list of affected disciplines. Economics, law, advertising and PR are fields where the labour market has signalled saturation for years and where demand for places has historically been sustained by households rather than by employers. By shrinking the paid channel, the state removes the safety valve that allowed supply in these disciplines to keep growing regardless of graduate employment outcomes.

The loan policy works in the same direction. Since last year, subsidised education loans are unavailable for many non-priority directions; the preferential state-supported loan at 3% has been preserved for engineering, IT, medical, pedagogical and other spheres important to the economy. A family that still wants a paid place in jurisprudence now faces both a smaller number of seats and the full market cost of financing them.

The quota ladder, rewired

The second structural change concerns the order in which budget places move between admission categories. In addition to olympiad winners, free study is available to applicants admitted under quotas; collectively they can occupy no more than 20% of all budget places, Filin notes. Within that envelope, the sequence has changed.

"This year the order of distributing budget places changed. If, after admission under the special quota — for applicants with disabilities — and the target quota, free places remain, they now pass first of all into the separate quota — for children of servicemen. Two or three years ago those places returned to the general competition," Filin explains. Some universities, he adds, enlarge the separate quota further: instead of the obligatory 10%, they admit up to 20% of applicants under it, and the ministry supports such initiatives.

For an ordinary applicant with high scores, the change narrows the residual pool of places in the general competition. Every unfilled quota place now has a designated next destination before the open list ever sees it.

Olympiad winners, 100-pointers and the density of competition

The 2026 cycle also intensified competition from the top of the score distribution. The number of schoolchildren who scored 100 points on the EGE rose to almost 9,000, Rosobrnadzor said. Dmitry Burakov (Дмитрий Бураков), founder of the education project VvuzZ, describes how this density concentrates at a small number of institutions: every year the top universities run very high competition for budget places, attracting privileged categories, applicants under special and separate quotas, olympiad winners and graduates with maximum scores — 300 and above including individual achievements. In ordinary, non-top universities, by contrast, there may be no olympiad winners for years at a time.

The olympiad channel itself is about to tighten. Last week the Ministry of Education announced stricter rules for participants of the all-Russian schoolchildren olympiad: winners and prizewinners may be barred from advancing to the municipal, regional and final stages on the strength of past-year merits. The scale of the channel is small relative to the cohort: in 2026 diplomas went to 4,000 schoolchildren, while roughly 650,000 people received 11th-grade certificates.

Target places learn the names of employers

The most institutionally significant reform of the cycle began earlier, in April, when the government distributed target-training quotas for the upcoming campaign under new rules — Kommersant reported. Previously, allocating the quota, the government indicated only the percentage it should occupy within the total budget places for each direction. Now, for every university, a fixed number of places is written down for each direction, bound to a concrete employer and region. Before the campaign even started, an applicant could see which enterprise stands behind a given place and where the graduate is expected to work.

How the target mechanism works

  • Target-place students sign a contract simultaneously with the university and the future employer.
  • After receiving the diploma, graduates must work three to five years at the contracted enterprise.
  • Their places are financed from the federal budget and admitted through a separate competition within the quota.
  • Since 2024, all employers are obliged to post their requests on the "Work in Russia" portal, where applicants can see the number of vacancies and apply.

In response to a Kommersant query, the Ministry of Science and Higher Education said about 83,000 places were distributed in total: more than 35,000 for bachelor's programmes, 26,000 for specialist degrees, 4,000 for master's programmes and 16,000 for higher-qualification training programmes. The approaches were changed on the president's instruction.

Prime Minister Mikhail Mishustin (Михаил Мишустин) framed the mechanism as a three-way guarantee: it will "fully take into account the personnel needs of regions, guarantee the graduate a workplace in their profile, and give the company a trained and motivated specialist." The ministry adds that the new rules exclude the risk of employers posting offers for the same target places and let applicants see customer demand more than two months before admission begins.

Why the old system kept failing

The reform answers a specific dysfunction. In past years, employers filed requests for target places that had already been allocated, and it regularly turned out that offers from customers were fewer than the quotas reserved for them. The result was unfilled target places — and blame assigned to universities. Representatives of the rectors' community complained that they were held responsible for the unpopularity of target places at their institutions. The new order inverts the sequence: the place exists only because a named employer asked for it, so an unfilled quota now points at the employer's offer rather than at the university's marketing.

The change also shifts the burden of attraction. Alexey Itin (Алексей Итин), head of the admissions department at ITMO University, observes that it will now be possible to track how actively enterprises work to attract applicants before admissions begin. Relying on being found through the "Work in Russia" portal will no longer be enough, he argues: the employer's responsibility rises. For universities, the new rules potentially simplify administrative work, because it is immediately clear how many people applied and to which enterprises.

Transparency versus student interest

Experts acknowledge the direction of travel while questioning its limits. Evgeny Terentiev (Евгений Терентьев), director of the Institute of Education at HSE University, notes that the new approach will stimulate negotiations between universities and employers. But difficulties with planning workplaces several years ahead cannot be avoided, he cautions: the outcome "depends on the conditions the employer offers and on the interests of students, which do not always move in the same direction as the labour market."

Boris Ilyukhin (Борис Илюхин), senior research fellow at the Centre for Economics of Continuous Education at RANEPA, is more upbeat: employers are the parties most interested in the detailed binding, because it lets them plan staffing more precisely and count on selecting the strongest applicants within the competition. Over recent years, he says, the approach to target training has become "noticeably more transparent and more manageable," and the new rules will make recruitment more effective and competitive.

The two positions mark the real boundary of the reform. The state can name the employer, fix the number of places and enforce the three-to-five-year work obligation. It cannot make a cohort of seventeen-year-olds want the jobs those places describe — which is why the parallel instruments of the campaign, from paid-place caps to loan restrictions, work on the structure of choices rather than on preferences.

What families experienced

At the household level, the campaign added procedural friction on top of the structural changes. Olga Kreslavskaya (Ольга Креславская), the mother of an applicant, told Kommersant FM that technical failures compounded the stress. Individual achievements that give extra admission points were not immediately reflected at all universities; only after about a week did all the data display correctly. The problem, the family was told, is that the systems are not fully synchronised: documents are submitted through Gosuslugi and then transferred to the university, where part of the information does not appear automatically, has to be entered manually and sent back into the system. Throughout, the university kept in touch by email.

Her son's experience also shows that the tightening is selective. The family applied to technical, physics-related directions that the state currently prioritises, and in all the universities where documents were submitted there were ample budget places — "not two, not three and not five places." The scarcity concentrated in the prestigious universities and the humanities-and-management directions that olympiad winners and quota applicants fill first. As for the widespread belief that children of special-military-operation participants occupy practically all budget places, Burakov stresses that it does not correspond to reality — although the theme recurs every campaign because it is emotionally charged for applicants and parents.

The key numbers of the cycle

  • 47,000 paid places cut — about 13% of the total — mainly in economics, advertising and PR, and jurisprudence.
  • About 83,000 target places distributed with binding to employer and region: 35,000+ bachelor's, 26,000 specialist, 4,000 master's, 16,000 higher-qualification.
  • Up to 20% of all budget places may be occupied by quota admissions; some universities admit up to 20% under the separate quota instead of the obligatory 10%.
  • Almost 9,000 schoolchildren scored 100 points on the EGE, up sharply year on year.
  • 4,000 all-Russian olympiad diplomas against roughly 650,000 11th-grade certificates.
  • A 3% state-subsidised education loan remains available only for engineering, IT, medical, pedagogical and other priority spheres.
  • July 25 was the final day of document submission; results were published on July 27.

What the new configuration means

Read together, the changes describe a coherent model. The state finances the places it wants — engineering, medicine, teaching, IT — and increasingly buys them forward, with a named employer and a three-to-five-year work obligation attached. It shrinks the channels through which households could previously purchase places in saturated disciplines, both by capping paid intake and by withdrawing the subsidised loan. It orders the quota ladder so that unfilled places cascade toward socially prioritised categories before the general competition. And it tightens the olympiad channel at the top, where the density of perfect scores made admission outcomes hardest to read.

For universities, the model reduces freedom and increases predictability: commercial intake in non-priority fields can no longer be expanded at will, but target places arrive pre-matched with employers, and administrative work around quotas becomes simpler. For employers, the reform converts target training from a passive entitlement into an active recruitment obligation — a place exists only if someone is prepared to offer a graduate work for three to five years and compete for the strongest candidates. For families, the practical conclusion of the 2026 campaign is planning: the rules of the ladder — which quota moves where, which directions carry loans, how many places sit behind each employer — now matter as much as the score itself.

The disputes that accompanied the closing days of the campaign — about olympiad winners crowding out high scorers, about quota priorities, about who really occupies the budget places — will return next summer. But the framework in which they are argued has changed. Admissions in Russia are no longer only a competition of scores; they are the yearly settlement of an argument about which occupations the state is willing to finance, and the 2026 cycle answered that question more explicitly than any before it.
